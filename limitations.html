Limitations

Every claim on this site rests on two haplogroup assignments and a body of published population genetics. Both have real limits, and those limits are worth stating plainly — a reader who knows what the evidence cannot show is better placed to judge what it can.

Last updated: 6 September 2026

Two lines out of more than a thousand

A Y-DNA haplogroup traces one single path: father, to his father, to his father, and so on. An mtDNA haplogroup traces the equivalent path through mothers. Neither says anything about any other ancestor.

Ten generations back — roughly 250 to 300 years — a person has up to 1,024 ancestral slots. Y-DNA describes exactly one of those lines. mtDNA describes exactly one more. The other 1,022 lines are invisible to both tests. A great-grandmother's father, for instance, contributes to who someone is and appears in neither result.

This is the single most important thing to understand about the evidence presented here. E-PF2546 and H1-T16189C! are real, specific and informative — about two threads. They are not a summary of ancestry.

A haplogroup is not an ethnicity, a nationality or a tribe

Haplogroups are branches on a human family tree defined by mutations. They are far older than any modern nation, and older than most named peoples. E-M81 is often called "the Berber marker" as convenient shorthand, but the label describes a statistical association with Amazigh populations, not a definition of who is Amazigh.

Carrying E-M81 does not make someone Amazigh, and not carrying it does not make someone less so. Amazigh identity is carried by language, culture, self-designation and community — Tachelhit spoken in a household does more to make that identity real than any marker on a chromosome.

Tribal and confederation membership, including Chtouka affiliation, is likewise a social and historical fact. It is recorded in genealogy, custom and place, not in a test result.

The dates carry wide error bars

Coalescence dates (TMRCA) on this site are estimates, not measurements. They depend on which mutation rate is used for calibration, how many samples were sequenced, and which populations those samples came from. Different published methods produce genuinely different answers from the same data.

The clearest example is on this site already: the parent clade E-M81 has a debated TMRCA of roughly 2,000 to 4,200 years ago (D'Atanasio et al. 2018). That is a spread of more than two millennia — it spans the difference between a Roman-era expansion and a much older one. Where a single figure appears here, it is the midpoint of a range, and the range is what the evidence actually supports.

The same applies to the H1 arrival estimate of approximately 11,400 years ago, whose published range runs from about 9,000 to 13,700 years (Ottoni et al. 2010).

One sample cannot describe a population

An individual result is a single data point. It can confirm that a lineage exists in a region; it cannot establish how common that lineage is, how it is distributed, or how it arrived. Every population-level claim on this site comes from the published studies listed under Research & Sources, never from the personal result.

Where those two kinds of evidence appear near each other, the published work is doing the work of generalisation.

North African ancient DNA is still a thin record

Ancient genomes from the Maghreb remain scarce compared with Europe, and preservation conditions across much of North Africa are poor. Several conclusions summarised here rest on small sample counts from a handful of sites.

This is a field where the picture has changed substantially within the last decade and can be expected to change again. Findings described here as established should be read as currently best supported. Where studies genuinely disagree, this site tries to say so rather than pick a winner.

What this site does claim

That two specific haplogroups sit in one Souss-Massa family line; that the published literature places those haplogroups in a particular historical and geographic context; and that the cultural material here reflects the Chtouka confederation as it is lived and remembered.

It does not claim to have measured anyone's ancestry, resolved a debated date, or spoken for Amazigh people generally.
